# From Experience to Encoding

The chart follows two connected arguments. First, cultural patterns move between thought, voiced expression, and embodied or material expression: psychomeme, phonomeme, phenomeme. Second, phenomemetic forms can be compared by the dimensions through which their organization is encountered. A performed event unfolds in space and time (4D); an object presents volume (3D); an image organizes a plane (2D); a symbolic code organizes a sequence (1D).

This ordering is the present project's conceptual framework. People continue to think, speak, perform, build, draw, and write together. A particular cultural lineage can omit categories, branch into several forms, or feed back into a performance. The chart's arrows express a specified relationship; they do not establish historical ancestry without a source.

Reduction makes a selection. A wire shaped after a movement can preserve its trajectory while omitting its timing and effort. A drawing of the wire can preserve a projected outline while omitting its other sides. An ordered description can preserve selected relationships while depending on a convention that makes those relationships recoverable. The dimensional number alone neither measures the richness of an experience nor sets a maximum on what a code can describe.

The distinction between encoding and carrier is essential. Writing is organized as a sequence even when it is laid out across a page, bound in a book, and held by a reader. A digital file likewise has a physical carrier. The dimension shown here is an analytical choice about the representation, rather than a claim that the carrier has lost its other dimensions. A facsimile image and a text transcription can therefore receive different classifications even when both are stored as digital files.

Fidelity needs an object. The notebook's concern with a thought passing through another consciousness differs from the question of whether a scribe preserves words, and both differ from whether a fixed digital file is copied exactly. The model separates what a transition preserves, omits, and enables. It does not assign a single fidelity score. Durability and distribution similarly depend on materials, maintenance, institutions, and decoding conventions; there is no automatic longevity ranking attached to the dimensions.

Paradise Lost is a useful bounded case. The Morgan documents a Book I fair copy, probably assembled from dictated materials, corrected under Milton's direction, and used as printer's copy for the 1667 edition. Dartmouth identifies the ten-book first edition and the revised twelve-book edition of 1674. Oxford identifies an EPUB text based on a 1674 source edition. These sources establish artifacts and relationships, while the thought node, dimensional assignments, and claims about experiential loss remain interpretations. The example distinguishes editions and an identified digital source without inventing a single unbroken provenance for every copy.

The reusable unit is a sourced graph: forms are nodes, transformations are edges, and provenance belongs to each claim. The same rendering system can then examine an oral narrative, a design, a ritual, a map, or a codebase. Its value is in making comparisons explicit enough to inspect and revise.
